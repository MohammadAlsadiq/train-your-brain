01 / Recall & illusions of competence

Familiar doesn’t always mean learned.

Reading a solution can feel effortless because the answer is right in front of you. Closing it and reconstructing the idea reveals what you can retrieve independently. Use that gap to decide what to practice next. [2]

The code-reading trap

You follow a binary-search tutorial and every line makes sense. Tomorrow, can you explain the algorithm and implement it on a blank page? Try recalling first, then compare with a reliable solution and correct mistakes.

Boxing analogy: recognizing a combination in a demonstration is different from producing it during practice. This is an analogy, not evidence that studying and sport work identically.

Make it a habit

After a short study block, close your notes. Write the main ideas, solve one problem, and check your work. Revisit the errors rather than only rereading the easy parts.

Try it / a tiny recall experiment

Study these three ideas, then hide them:

  • Retrieve: bring an idea to mind without looking.
  • Check: compare your attempt with a reliable answer.
  • Repair: correct the gap and try again later.

Retrieve → Check → Repair. Recall without notes, compare with an answer, then correct errors and revisit later.

An illustration of recall and confidence, not a scientific measurement of your memory.

02 / Spaced practice

Return to it. Give it time.

Spread practice across separate sessions instead of putting every repetition into one sitting. In each review, attempt recall before checking your notes. Adjust the interval when recall is too difficult or too easy. [3]

Build a review plan

Example intervals: 1, 3, 7, and 14 days after first study. These are a starting point, not a universally optimal schedule.

03 / Procrastination & process

Don’t finish everything. Start something.

An intimidating outcome—“finish the whole chapter”—can make starting harder. Set a short process goal: “work on one problem for 25 minutes.” Remove a distraction, work until the timer ends, then take a break. The course presents the Pomodoro technique as one way to begin. [1]

A simple starting ritual

  1. Notice the cue: a difficult task makes you reach for your phone.
  2. Change the routine: put the phone away and open one problem.
  3. Work for a bounded time: focus on the process, not completing the whole assignment.
  4. Take a small reward: step away for a brief break.

A timer supports a routine; it does not guarantee concentration or learning. End the session by recalling what you worked on.

04 / Focused & diffuse thinking · Chunking · Interleaving

Choose how to practice.

The course uses focused and diffuse modes as a simplified way to describe concentrated work and broader thinking. First engage with a problem; a break may help you return with a different approach. It is not a switch you can control precisely. [1]

Build a chunk, then learn when to use it

A chunk is a meaningful set of connected ideas you can use together. In coding, understand a worked example, reconstruct the method without looking, and practice applying it. Memorizing lines without understanding their purpose is a fragile shortcut.

Once you understand individual methods, mix related problem types. This is interleaving: practice choosing a method as well as executing it. For example, mix search and traversal tasks instead of labeling every task “use binary search.” [1]

    Putting it into my life

    From the classroom to the training room.

    I am a computer science student and an amateur boxer. I chose an interactive website to connect learning techniques with situations that matter to me and to make them useful to other learners.

    In my study plan, I will explain algorithms from memory, revisit difficult concepts across several days, and use short focus sessions to make large assignments easier to start. Boxing gives me a useful analogy for deliberate practice: understanding an explanation is only the beginning.

    This section describes my intended application. It does not report completed experiments or measured improvements.

    My seven-day application plan

    • Day 1: study one concept; explain it without notes and record gaps.
    • Day 2: retrieve it again and correct errors.
    • Day 4: mix it with a related problem type.
    • Day 7: attempt a new application without a worked solution.

    At each attempt, record what I could explain independently and which mistakes returned. I will use that record to adjust my next review.

    Project overview for peer reviewers

    This project is an English-language interactive learning guide for students and independent learners. It applies recall, illusions of competence, spaced practice, procrastination and process goals, focused/diffuse thinking, chunking, and interleaving. Activities illustrate these concepts; they are not controlled experiments. References appear below.
